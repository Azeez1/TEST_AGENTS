System Design · Lesson 1

The one fact the whole field rests on

Before any component, one bedrock truth. Everything else is built on top of it.

You already know words like load balancer, cache, and sharding. The problem with knowing the words is they feel like a random toolbox — a pile of parts with no logic connecting them. This lesson hands you the logic. After it, those parts stop being a vocabulary list and become a family tree, all descended from one ancestor.

Picture this first

Imagine a tiny corner shop with one cashier. When two customers walk in, fine. When two hundred walk in at once, the line spills out the door. The cashier didn't get worse — there's just more work than one person can handle. Every fix you can imagine (hire a second cashier, add a self-checkout, keep popular items by the register) is a response to one cashier not being enough.

A computer is that cashier. And here is the whole foundation of system design:

The floor — the irreducible truth

A single machine has hard limits, and it can fail. The moment your job is bigger than one machine, you must use more — and using more than one machine is what creates every problem (and every component) in system design.

That's it. That's the ancestor. Let's break the floor into its pieces, because each piece spawns a different branch of the family.

What "limits" actually means

A single computer can only offer four finite resources, plus one inconvenient fact about reality. When your need crosses any of these, you've hit a wall — and a named tool exists to climb it.

The limitPlain meaningThe wall you hit
Compute (CPU)How much thinking per second.Too many requests; the machine maxes out and slows to a crawl.
Memory (RAM)How much it can hold "in hand" at once, fast.Data set won't fit; the machine starts thrashing or crashes.
Storage (Disk)How much it can keep long-term.You run out of room to store the data.
ReliabilityOne machine can and will break.It dies at 3am and everything goes down with it.
LatencyTalking to another machine takes time — info can't travel faster than light.Spreading work across machines adds delay you can't erase.

The first four are about one machine not being enough. The fifth — latency — is the tax you pay the instant you add a second machine to fix the first four. That tension is the entire game: you add machines to escape limits, and adding machines creates a new limit. (This framing — reliability, scalability, maintainability as the root concerns — is the opening argument of Kleppmann's Designing Data-Intensive Applications.)

Now watch the tools fall out of the floor

Here's the punchline. Every component you've heard of is just a named response to one specific limit above. Not best practice. Not tradition. A direct answer to a wall.

Limit hitThe responseThe tool's name
Compute maxedAdd more machines, spread requests across themLoad balancer + more servers
Same work repeatedKeep the answer close so you don't recompute itCache
Storage too big for one diskSplit the data across machinesSharding / partitioning
One machine can failKeep copies on other machinesReplication
Work can wait / spikesPut tasks in a line to handle laterQueue

You don't memorize this table. You derive it. Asked "why a cache?", you don't say "because it's fast" — you say "because recomputing the same answer wastes the compute limit, so we store it once." That's first-principles reasoning, and it's exactly what makes a system-design interview (or a client conversation) sound like understanding instead of recall.

Fact vs. choice

Keep two buckets straight as we go through this whole mission:

Bedrock fact: a single machine has finite CPU/RAM/disk, it can fail, and cross-machine talk costs latency. These are not negotiable.

Choice / trade-off: which tool you reach for, and what you sacrifice to use it (a cache can serve stale data; replication can disagree with itself). These could always have gone another way — and the "right" answer depends on the situation.

Quick check — instant feedback

Match the symptom to the underlying limit. Click an answer.

1. Your app re-runs the exact same expensive database query thousands of times a minute and the servers are pinned at 100%. Which limit is the root cause a cache would relieve?

Right. A cache exists because recomputing a known answer burns the compute limit. Store it once, reuse it. The cost/choice: that stored answer can go stale.
Not quite. The servers being pinned at 100% is the compute wall — you're spending CPU to recompute something you already figured out. That's what a cache fixes.

2. Your single database server holds everything. One night its disk dies and the whole product goes offline. Which limit just bit you?

Exactly. This is the reliability limit. The named response is replication — keep copies on other machines so one death isn't fatal.
Look again — nothing here is about being slow or full. A machine broke. That's the reliability limit, and replication is its answer.

3. You split your data across 10 machines to fit it all. Now a request that needs data from 3 of them is noticeably slower than before. What did you trade away?

Yes — this is the whole tension. You added machines to beat the storage limit and inherited the latency tax. There's no free lunch; design is choosing which tax to pay.
Reconsider. You solved storage by splitting — but now requests hop between machines, and each hop costs time. That's the latency tax for going multi-machine.
