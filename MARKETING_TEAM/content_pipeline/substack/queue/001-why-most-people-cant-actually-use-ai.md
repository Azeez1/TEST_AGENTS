# Why Most People Can't Actually Use AI (It's Not What You Think)

Your AI is the same as everyone else's. Same model, same prompts, same subscription.

So why are some people 10x-ing their output with it and most aren't?

Not because they have better prompts. Not because they paid for the better tier. Not because they're "more technical."

Because most people aren't failing at AI. They're failing at learning. And AI is just the sharpest possible tool for exposing that.

## The Real Problem Is A Brain Problem

AI adoption is not a software problem. It's a cognitive problem. And cognitive problems look like software problems to people who've never studied how cognition actually works.

Watch what happens: someone asks ChatGPT to "summarize this report," reads the summary, closes the task. Feels productive. Dopamine fires. Done.

What happened inside their head? Nothing.

They didn't [[Encoding|encode]] any of it. They didn't build a schema. They didn't connect it to anything they already knew. Their brain was a pipe — information went in one side as PDF, came out the other side as a Slack message. Zero of it stuck. Zero of it compounds.

This is the [[Illusion of Learning]] — confusing *understanding in the moment* with *learning*. It was always a trap. AI just makes it infinitely smoother and more addictive.

## The Adopter's Illusion

Someone "adopts AI." Runs everything through it — emails, summaries, research, explanations. Six months in, reports feeling 10x more productive.

Then a real problem shows up. A client asks a question the AI can't answer. A meeting shifts to a domain where no prompt helps. A project needs a judgment call based on context that isn't in any dataset.

And they have nothing. Not because AI failed — because for six months they were a pipe. Same capability as day one. Possibly less, because the muscles they stopped using have atrophied.

This is [[Cognitive Offloading Damage]]. Not that offloading is bad — writing your grocery list down is fine. But when you offload the *processing* itself, not just the *holding*, you stop building expertise. Every summary you consume instead of produce is a rep you didn't take. Compound that over months and you have a worker who feels productive, moves fast, and falls apart the moment the AI can't help.

## The Test Nobody Passes

Run this diagnostic on yourself. Weekly. It's brutal and it's fair.

> Walk into a room with someone else using the same AI you use. Can you produce higher-quality output than them?
>
> If yes: your edge is real — the AI is a multiplier on top of capability you built.
>
> If no: your edge is the AI. And the AI is commoditized. Which means you have no edge at all.

Most people fail this test because they never built the underlying capability. They adopted the tool without adopting the work. They skipped encoding.

## The Gym Analogy Is The Whole Game

Learning works like weight training. If the weight feels heavy, your muscles are getting stronger. If it feels light, they aren't.

This is [[Desirable Difficulty]] — 40 years of research out of UCLA, Robert Bjork's work. The discomfort of struggle is not a bug in the process. It's the *signal* that the process is working.

The terrifying thing about AI is that it engineers that discomfort out of every cognitive task.

You don't struggle to understand — AI rephrases until it clicks.
You don't struggle to remember — AI retrieves.
You don't struggle to decide — AI recommends.

Every one of those struggles was where the learning actually happened. All of it, gone.

You feel smoother, faster, more productive. And quietly, you are becoming worse.

## The People Who Are Winning

The ones getting 10x from AI right now aren't the ones offloading everything. They're the ones who already had deep capability and are using AI to [[AI as Assistant Not Brain|assist it, not replace it]].

They use AI as a [[AI as Scoping Tool|scoping tool]]. Map the territory fast. Interrogate their thinking for gaps. Then go do the actual work themselves — with their own brain, real sources, their own encoding.

They treat AI like a research assistant who doesn't get tenure. Fast help, valuable help. But the thinking stays with the human who owns the decision.

The ones losing treat it like a ghostwriter for their own mind.

## Why Companies Are Getting This Wrong

Every company I've walked into in the last year has the same pitch deck slide: "We're implementing AI across our workflows to unlock 10x productivity."

Then you look at their actual rollout. It's ChatGPT licenses. Some Slack integration. Maybe an internal RAG agent. And they measure success by "how many people are using it daily."

That's not AI adoption. That's distribution. Distribution of a tool is easy. Adoption of a tool means the humans using it are producing better outputs because of it — not the same outputs faster.

The companies doing this right are asking a different question entirely. They're asking: **"Where in our workflow does AI build our people's capability, and where does it replace it?"**

Because if AI is replacing capability, the first year looks like productivity and the third year looks like an organization that's forgotten how to do the work. A company full of people who can prompt but can't think. Who can execute with AI but can't catch an AI error because they never built the underlying expertise.

That's not productivity. That's leverage at the cost of durability — the exact inverse of what compounding looks like.

## What To Actually Do

If this is stinging — good. That's the signal your brain was about to offload this essay to summary-mode. Stop. Answer these in your head, not to ChatGPT:

1. **What have I learned this month that I could explain on a whiteboard with no tools?**
2. **What problems did I solve where AI couldn't help me — and how does that work look compared to six months ago?**
3. **Am I using AI to do work faster, or to avoid work that would have built my capability?**

If those questions make you uncomfortable, that discomfort is [[Desirable Difficulty]] showing up right on cue. Your brain knows what's happening.

## The Frame That Fixes This

For every AI interaction, run one filter. I call it the **Build-or-Replace Test**:

**Is this AI use building my capability, or replacing it?**

- Draft I'll rewrite from scratch → *building.* The rewrite forces encoding.
- Draft I'll lightly edit and ship → *replacing.* Outsourced thinking.
- Explaining a concept I'll then teach to someone else → *building.* Feynman catches gaps.
- Explaining a concept I'll nod along to → *replacing.* Classic illusion of learning.
- Summarizing a doc so I can engage deeply in the meeting → *building,* if I actually engage.
- Summarizing a doc so I can reply without reading → *replacing.* Pipe mode.

The tool isn't the problem. The relationship to the tool is.

## The Bottom Line

AI adoption isn't about the AI. It's about whether the human using it is still doing the work that makes them valuable.

The winners over the next decade aren't the ones who adopted fastest. They're the ones who adopted in a way that made them stronger, not lighter. They kept their [[Encoding|encoding]] loop intact. They kept the friction where the friction mattered.

If you're treating AI like a ghostwriter for your own mind, you don't have an AI problem. You have a capability problem — and AI just made it invisible for longer than it otherwise would have been.

It's fixable in a month of deliberate work. Most people won't do it, because the comfortable path is right there and the AI is happy to keep them on it.

Choose the friction.
